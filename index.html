Δfmax is the Doppler shift when the satellite crosses the horizon (maximum radial velocity). The peak Doppler rate occurs at culmination. A 550 km pass at UHF sweeps roughly ±10 kHz at up to ~100 Hz/s — that is why LEO ground stations need continuous frequency tracking, while GEO satellites need almost none.

Formulas & derivation summary

  • r = RE + h, with RE = 6371 km, μ = 3.986004418×10¹⁴ m³/s²
  • v = √(μ/r) · T = 2π√(r³/μ) · n = v/r (orbital rate)
  • Geometry (ground station at zenith angle): ρ² = R² + r² − 2Rr·cosψ
  • cosψ = [ R·cos²E + sinE·√(r² − R²cos²E) ] / r  (E = elevation)
  • Radial velocity: vrad = v·R·sinψ / ρ (approach = positive)
  • Max radial velocity at E = 0°: vrad,max = v·R/r (since ρ = √(r²−R²) there)
  • Δf = fc·vrad/c → Δfmax = fc·v·(R/r)/c
  • Doppler rate: dΔf/dt = (fc/c)·v·n·(R/r)·g(ψ)/A(ψ)^(3/2), peak at culmination
  •    A(ψ) = 1 + (R/r)² − 2(R/r)cosψ, g(ψ) = (1+(R/r)²)cosψ − (R/r)cos²ψ − (R/r)
  • Pass duration above horizon: Tpass = 2·(arccos(R/r) − ψculm)/n

The often-quoted rough estimate Δf = fc·v/c (no geometry) overstates the horizon Doppler by ~8–9 % for LEO; the R/r factor above is the standard correction. The satellite speed direction is perpendicular to its position vector (circular orbit), and the ground station is assumed stationary in the orbit plane — the geometry matches any pass projected onto the observer. Real TLE-based propagators (SGP4) add inclination and azimuth effects; this tool gives the worst-case/canonical pass for budget and receiver planning.

Why LEO Doppler is so large

  • Speed × frequency. A LEO satellite moves at ~7.5 km/s. At UHF (437 MHz) that alone is ~10.9 kHz of Doppler; at X band (8.1 GHz) it is ~200 kHz — thousands of times wider than the Doppler of a GEO satellite, which barely moves relative to the ground.
  • Geometry compresses the timescale. The radial velocity swings from +v·(R/r) at acquisition to −v·(R/r) at loss within a 10–15 minute pass, and it changes fastest when the satellite is at culmination — the peak rate reaches ~140 Hz/s at UHF for a 550 km overhead pass.
  • Narrow channels feel it most. A 1.2 kHz NB-IoT or amateur CW channel is completely wiped out by an uncorrected UHF Doppler; even a 1 MHz broadband channel sees a 200 kHz sweep at S band, so every LEO receiver design starts from this table.

Receiver frequency-tracking design guidance

  1. Size the search/tuning span. Pre-correct with two-line-element (TLE) predictions and still reserve a search window of at least ±(Δfmax + prediction error); TLE-based residual error after a day or two is typically a few hundred Hz to a few kHz at UHF.
  2. Close the loop fast enough. The loop bandwidth must handle the peak rate (hundreds of Hz/s at UHF, several kHz/s at X band): a classic choice is a Type-2/Type-3 FLL-assisted PLL, or open-loop Doppler pre-correction from the pass prediction plus a narrow residual PLL.
  3. Rate, not just offset, drives the design. At culmination the frequency sweeps at maximum speed while the signal also peaks; if your loop can track the rate at culmination it can track the whole pass.
  4. Do the arithmetic in the right units. Doppler scales linearly with carrier frequency — compute once at a reference frequency and scale, but verify oscillator references (TCXO vs OCXO vs rubidium) since ground-station reference error adds directly to the residual.
